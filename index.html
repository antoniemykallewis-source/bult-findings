01 / The findings

Trust comes first.

The goal: a steady 10,000 active subscribers. Not 10k new subscribers each month. That means retention, or keeping the people who already subscribe, matters a lot. Most of these fixes build trust and give people a reason to stay past month two.

01 / Refund policy

The refund policy argues with the guarantee.

What we saw

The product page says "30 days, no question asked, keep the pouches, no return shipping." But the refund policy asks for unused items in their original packaging. It also says "[INSERT RETURN ADDRESS]" and sends late-refund questions to a Gmail address.

Why it matters for subs

A clear guarantee can help a cautious buyer try the first month. If the policy says the opposite, they may not buy. Or they may lose trust before the next payment.

The fix

Make the refund policy match the guarantee. Fill in the return address. Replace the Gmail address with support@bult.com.

02 / Terms of service

Terms of Service still has template blanks.

What we saw

The Terms page still has blanks from the Shopify template, such as "[LINK]." It also calls the store "getbult."

Why it matters for subs

Buyers may check the fine print before agreeing to repeat charges. Empty fields can make the store look unfinished.

The fix

Fill every blank. Use the real business name. Have a lawyer check the finished page.

03 / Science links

Some Science page links go to the wrong papers.

What we saw

At least 6 of the 28 "View Study" links open research on other topics. B6 links to a coffee review. Folate links to acupuncture and IVF. B12 links to an EPA depression trial. Copper links to amino acids and obesity. Chromium links to carotenoids in newborns. Muira Puama links to "The Full Monty and life-long learning."

Why it matters for subs

This page is meant to back up the brand's claims. A buyer who checks a link may find the wrong study. That can weaken their trust and their reason to stay.

The fix

Check all 28 PMIDs, the ID numbers for papers in PubMed, against the right ingredients. Fix the links. Add a "last reviewed" date.

04 / Buy once flow

"Buy once" sends you back to the subscription.

What we saw

The "Buy once" button at the bottom of the product page jumps to the top. The subscription option is still selected there.

Why it matters for subs

We did not test checkout, so we have not shown that checkout is broken. But this jump may confuse someone who wants one order. Feeling pushed into repeat payments can lead to complaints or a quick cancel. A happy one-time buyer may choose to subscribe later.

The fix

Make "Buy once" select the one-time option, or add that option straight to the cart. Make the buyer's choice clear on the button.

05 / Results timeline

The two pages give different timelines.

What we saw

The homepage's "90 Day Foundation" uses days 1-30, 30-60 and 60-90+. The product page uses days 1-45, 45-90 and 90+.

Why it matters for subs

This tells buyers when to expect results. Those expectations may shape whether they stay or cancel. The two pages should agree.

The fix

Choose one timeline. Use it on both pages and in emails sent after a purchase.

06 / Blend transparency

"No proprietary blends" has an exception.

What we saw

The product page says "No proprietary blends. No hidden doses." A proprietary blend is a mix that does not show the dose of each ingredient. FAQ #24 says the Prebiotic and Probiotic Blend "is the one exception."

Why it matters for subs

Clear ingredient details are part of the pitch. The exception should be easy to find, not buried in the FAQ.

The fix

Put the exception beside the claim. For example: "No proprietary blends, except our probiotic, and here's every strain."

07 / Welcome kit

The welcome kit is hard to find.

What we saw

The FAQ has questions about a Welcome Kit and an aluminum dispenser. The subscription offer box does not mention either one.

Why it matters for subs

If the kit is included today, saying so could give buyers another reason to subscribe. Right now, the offer box leaves that question open.

The fix

First confirm that the kit is available and who can get it. If it is included, add a clear line and photo to the offer box. Include any limits or conditions.

08 / Pricing story

The price has changed since the press releases.

What we saw

The Apr 21 press release lists $100 for one order or $90 on subscription. The Oct 14 release says $90 a month. The site now says $112 for one order and $89 every 30 days.

Why it matters for subs

Someone who read an old release may expect the old price. These are past prices, not proof that today's price is broken. The one-time price is $12 higher than in the Apr 21 release.

The fix

Keep one clear, current price table on the site. Where the publisher allows it, add a dated update to older releases.

09 / Meta ads

The visible Meta ads are new.

What we saw

Meta Ad Library shows about 24 results for an exact-phrase search for "bult.com," filtered to active US ads. The six visible Bult ad cards started Oct 20 or Oct 23, 2026. This is one keyword search, not a full list of BULT's ads.

Why it matters for subs

These visible ads are recent. People who click paid ads may run into the site issues above. This public search does not show which ads bring subscribers who stay.

The fix

Add tracking tags to each ad. Check which ads bring people who stay past month two, not just people who place a first order.

02 / Competitive context

What’s worth borrowing.

These are three brands a BULT buyer may compare. We looked at their marketing and offers, not their formulas. The screenshots in proof/ were captured Oct 24, 2026.

10 / Competitive context

Onnit Total Human.

The offer + subscription

Total Human costs $137.95 for one order or $117.26 with "Subscribe & Save 15%." The page offers 30, 60 or 90 count supplies. It also shows a 30-day money-back guarantee and "HSA/FSA eligible," meaning it may qualify for those health spending accounts. There is a quiz banner and a popup offering 10% off for joining the email list.

The play worth borrowing

The ads repeat one clear idea: "No more clunky bottles... two simple packs per day." A search for "onnit.com," filtered to active US ads, showed about 37 results. Most visible Onnit ads started Oct 7. This is a keyword search, not a full count of Onnit's ads. The lesson is one clear hook, or main reason to buy, shown in many ways. Onnit also has 15,782 Trustpilot reviews with a 4.4 rating.

How BULT beats it

BULT can tell the same "packs not bottles" story with one pouch instead of two. Make that easy to see. Show the subscription savings as a clear percentage. Ask customers for honest reviews. Those reviews can help new buyers feel more sure about trying BULT.

11 / Competitive context

Persona Nutrition.

The offer + subscription

The banner says "2 Ways to Save: Save 20% or Buy 2, Get 1 Free." The top of the page offers a 5-minute quiz and "Talk to a nutritionist," a person who gives guidance about food and nutrients. It says packs are "built around your meds with 4,000+ interactions checked." That means checks for how ingredients may affect medicines. It also links to "10k+ reviews on Trustpilot."

The play worth borrowing

Persona makes human help part of the offer, with named nutritionists and their photos. Its ads describe the hassle of a daily routine: "It's not a willpower problem. It's a friction problem... the bottles just sit there." Here, friction means things that make a habit harder to keep. A search for "personanutrition.com," filtered to active US ads, showed about 19 results. This is not a full count of Persona's ads. Trustpilot shows 10,208 reviews with a 4.4 rating.

How BULT beats it

BULT's own press release quotes a urologist, a doctor who treats urinary and male reproductive health, and a registered dietitian, a trained food and nutrition expert. Make those existing quotes easier to find. An "ask our dietitian" option may be worth exploring, but only if an expert agrees and has time to help. Use the simpler-routine idea in ads and emails. One pouch a day is easy to explain. With one product, the offer can stay simple without a quiz.

12 / Competitive context

GNC Mega Men Vitapak.

The offer + subscription

Amazon lists Mega Men Whole Body Vitapak at $89.99 for 30 servings. It shows "Extra 5% off when you subscribe" and "1K+ bought in past month." That price is close to BULT's subscription price, with a small extra saving for repeat orders. GNC's own site blocked our browser, so this offer check is from Amazon only.

The play worth borrowing

GNC sells where people already shop. Its Vitapak range has options such as Whole Body, 50 Plus, 40+ and Performance. Those labels help buyers choose an option quickly. An exact-phrase Meta search for "gnc.com," filtered to active US ads, showed about 87 results. That is not a verified total of GNC's ads.

How BULT beats it

Make trust and service a reason to choose BULT. GNC's company-level Trustpilot page has a 1.5 rating from 270 reviews. The summary of those reviews mentions service and delivery problems. This is a rating of the company, not the product. These reviews do not show what all GNC customers think. BULT can offer a clear guarantee, a real person on email and complete policy pages. See findings 01 and 02.

The short version

Onnit has lots of reviews and one clear message. Persona makes expert help and an easier routine part of the pitch. GNC is easy to find where people shop. Its reviewed service complaints suggest a way for BULT to stand out. BULT can borrow the pouch message from Onnit and the expert-help and easier-routine ideas from Persona. Clear, helpful service can set it apart from the problems in GNC's review sample.

01

Show the first screen sooner.

Both pages load jQuery, Slick, Swiper and the subscription widget early. These are ready-made code tools, and they can hold up the first view. Load nonessential code later, in the right order. Use fewer overlapping slider tools and load only the layout rules needed for the first screen.

02

Let the product image arrive first.

The main product image is already in the page code. Tell the browser to fetch it first. Check whether the gallery or layout waits for other code before showing it. BULT already serves a smaller WebP version, a modern image format. Changing every image format is not the main fix.

03

Audit apps. Retest the buying flow.

Check code added by store apps. Some is not needed for the first screen, but it may be needed later, so test before removing it. Wait to load lower-page videos, not just pause them. Then retest subscription and one-time buying, add-to-cart, cart and the start of checkout. Repeat the phone and desktop speed tests.

These are proposed fixes. We have not changed BULT’s store. Test them in a theme preview first, then compare repeat runs under the same conditions. No score, time saving or sales increase is promised. Google’s LCP guidance ↗

04 / Recommendations

A retention-first path
to a steady 10k.

The plain-words math.

To hold 10,000 active subscribers, new signups must replace the people who cancel. Churn is the share of subscribers who cancel each month. Lower churn means fewer new signups are needed to hold the same total. You still need to win new buyers to reach 10,000 in the first place.

Example numbers only, not BULT's real data:

  • At 10% monthly churn, holding 10,000 subscribers takes about 1,000 new subscribers each month.
  • At 6% churn, it takes about 600 a month.
  • That is 40% fewer signups needed to replace cancellations. If each new subscriber costs the same to win, spending on those replacement signups falls by 40% too.

CAC means customer acquisition cost: the cost to win one new customer. Here we use media CAC, meaning ad spend divided by new subscribers won through those ads. It does not include all costs of running the business. With a constant churn rate, the average subscriber stays roughly 1 / churn months. Example: at 8% churn, that is about 12.5 months. Using the $89 price every 30 days as a simple stand-in for a monthly price gives about $1,100 in sales per subscriber before product cost. This leaves out the $69 first-shipment offer. Sales are not profit. Compare CAC with the money left after product, shipping and other order costs. Also check payback: how long it takes to earn back the cost of winning that subscriber.

Question for Alex: We don't know BULT's CAC yet. What does a new subscriber cost today? What share cancel each month? What is the margin, or share of sales left after product costs? Those numbers and payback help shape the plan.

What do CPA and CAC mean? +

Purchase CPA means ad spend divided by purchases counted by the ad platform. That may include one-time buyers or past customers. New-subscriber CAC counts only new customers whose first paid order starts a subscription. Count each person once. Do not count renewals, unpaid signups or past customers switching to a subscription.

Media CAC means ad spend only. All-in CAC adds the cost of making ads, agency help, affiliates and acquisition tools. Match the same spending dates, customer group and rules for which ad gets credit. For example, if 60% of purchases are new paying subscribers, a $40 purchase CPA means about $67 of ad spend per new subscriber. This is math, not BULT data. The other buyers may still earn money for the business.

Why $130
needs proof.

Gross margin is the share of sales left after the costs counted as product cost. Assume it is 70%; BULT’s real margin is unknown. One customer paying $69 leaves $48.30 of modeled gross profit. With $89 renewals, the total becomes $110.60 after two paid shipments and $172.90 after three.

That person must reach the third paid shipment to cover $130 with this modeled gross profit, before other costs. Payback means earning back what you spent to get the customer. This is not payback for a whole group: some people leave. It also does not show cash timing. Subtract shipping, packing, payment fees, refunds and other acquisition costs if the margin did not already include them.

Longer-term context / Hypothetical gross-margin model

What 10k takes.

The model below tests a set of guesses. It is not a launch ad budget. It keeps the same monthly cancel rate and uses gross profit at the renewal price. It leaves out the $69 first order. It cannot prove that a spending level is safe.

CAC in plain words.

CAC means customer acquisition cost: the cost to win one new customer. Here we use media CAC, meaning ad spend divided by new subscribers won through those ads. For example, spend $10,000 on ads and win 100 new subscribers, and media CAC is $100. CPA means cost per action. That action might be a sale, a lead or a signup. Check what action is being counted before comparing it with CAC.

Compare CAC with contribution, the money left after product, shipping and other order costs. Also check payback, or how long it takes to earn back the cost of winning a customer. LTV means lifetime value. The example below estimates lifetime gross profit: sales minus product cost over the time a subscriber stays. It leaves out costs beyond gross margin, so it does not show all the money available to spend on ads.

Worked example.

EVERY NUMBER BELOW IS AN EXAMPLE, NOT BULT DATA. The $89 price every 30 days was shown on bult.com, captured Oct 24, 2026. We use it as a simple stand-in for a monthly price. The $69 first-shipment offer is left out. We do not know BULT's churn, margin or CAC. Churn is the share of subscribers who cancel each month. Gross margin is the share of sales left after product cost.

Worked-example inputs
InputValue
Price$89 / 30 days (known price; monthly stand-in)
Gross marginEXAMPLE 70%
Monthly churnEXAMPLE 8%
Target active subs10,000

  1. 1. New subs needed each month to hold 10k

    10,000 x 8% = 800 new subscribers a month to replace the people who cancel. Retention means keeping existing subscribers. Better retention means fewer replacement signups are needed.

  2. 2. LTV

    $89 x 70% / 8% = about $779 in lifetime gross profit per subscriber. At a constant 8% monthly churn, a subscriber stays about 12.5 months on average. This estimate leaves out costs beyond gross margin.

  3. 3. CAC at an example 3:1 ratio

    A 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio means $3 in lifetime gross profit for each $1 spent to win a subscriber. In this example, $779 / 3 = about $260 per new subscriber. This is only a limit set by the example ratio. It is not a safe spending limit. Check payback, cash on hand and costs beyond gross margin before setting a budget.

  4. 4. Ad budget that implies

    800 x $260 = about $208,000 a month at the example ratio limit. Using a lower, hypothetical MEDIA CAC of $130 gives about $104,000. The $130 is an example, not BULT's measured cost.

  5. 5. Why churn matters so much (still EXAMPLE)

    If churn falls from 8% to 5%, holding 10,000 takes 500 new subscribers a month. Estimated LTV rises to about $1,246. At the example 3:1 ratio, the CAC limit rises to about $415. Keep the same hypothetical MEDIA CAC of $130, and the budget for replacing cancellations falls from $104,000 to $65,000. But if you spend up to each example's ratio limit instead, the budget stays roughly $208,000. Lower churn does not lower that ratio-limit budget.

  6. 6. Getting there from where you are now (EXAMPLE)

    Start with 500 subscribers. Add 1,500 new subscribers a month while 8% cancel each month. With those fixed rates, you cross 10,000 in about 9 months. Then 800 new subscribers a month would hold the total steady.
